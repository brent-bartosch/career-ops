The framework for the round
Here's how to think about the 30 minutes.
The CEO is testing three things in this order:

First 5-7 minutes: Can I talk to this person comfortably for 30 minutes? (The chemistry test.)
Middle 15-20 minutes: Does this person have a coherent worldview about the work and where they fit? (The conviction test.)
Last 5-7 minutes: Is this person going to stay, and do they understand what they'd be signing up for? (The durability test.)

Each phase has its own failure modes and its own optimal moves.
Phase 1: The Chemistry Test (first 5-7 minutes)
The opener matters disproportionately. Ron Gaver is direct (per Manisha), so expect a quick warm-up and then a substantive question fast. Likely opening sequence:

"Thanks for making time, I know you've been talking with the team."
"Tell me a bit about your background and what brings you to Tapcheck."

That second question is the highest-leverage moment of the entire round. It's wide open. It's also the question where you've historically slipped into the consultancy/exhaustion frame.
Have a 60-90 second answer locked. Not memorized verbatim like the consultancy answer, but structurally locked so you don't improvise. The shape:

One sentence on the career arc. "I came up through enterprise sales — Conversion Logic, Skai, Crealytics — quota-carrying for seven years on attribution and marketing intelligence platforms."
One sentence on the pivot. "Three years ago I started building the GTM systems I'd wished existed when I was selling. I needed to prove I could ship before any company would hire me for it, so I built a consultancy and shipped them — outbound engines, signal pipelines, attribution layers, account intelligence."
One sentence on why now. "Now I want to do that work inside one company, where it compounds over years instead of context-switching across clients."
One sentence on why Tapcheck. "Tapcheck specifically because the QSR motion fits how I think about TAM problems, and from the conversations I've had so far, the team is honest about what's working and what isn't — which is the environment where this kind of work actually gets adopted."

That answer does several things at once: establishes your seller-plus-engineer credential, reframes the consultancy as completion rather than exhaustion, signals durability, and ends on a specific reason for this company rather than generic enthusiasm. Total time: ~75 seconds. Then stop.
Practice this answer out loud Wednesday until it sounds natural. Not memorized — the goal is for the structure to be muscle memory while the words flow. If you stumble on phrasing, fine. If you forget the structure, you'll wander into exhaustion territory.
Phase 2: The Conviction Test (middle 15-20 minutes)
This is where the CEO will ask the questions that actually test you. Based on Manisha's notes, expect some combination of:

KPIs / metrics that you've used to assess what good looks like
Your experience coming into a role that's new to the company
Why you're interested in Tapcheck (deeper than the opener)
Direct questions about your background

He may also ask the harder, less predictable questions:

"What's the biggest risk this role fails in year 1?"
"What would you build first?"
"What's your honest read on where Tapcheck is in the market?"
"What questions do you have for me?"

For each of these, the discipline is the same: headline → one supporting fact → stop.
I want to walk through three of the highest-stakes ones specifically.
"What KPIs would you use to measure your impact?"
This is the question Manisha flagged directly. Don't list every metric you can think of — that signals over-thinking and lack of prioritization. Pick three, with reasoning.

"Three I'd anchor on, in priority order. First: lead-to-opportunity conversion rate by source, because that tells me whether the systems I'm building are actually improving pipeline quality rather than just adding volume. Second: per-rep weekly hours saved through automation, because that's the leading indicator of whether what I'm building is actually adopted. Third: time-to-first-meaningful-action for new reps, because if the systems work, ramp time should compress measurably. I'd want to baseline all three in the first 30 days before I build anything significant, because you can't claim impact without a baseline."

That's 60 seconds, structured, opinionated, ends on a discipline point (baseline before build). It also signals you think about adoption (not just shipping) and ramp (not just productivity), which are two things sales leaders specifically care about.
"Your experience coming into a role that's new to the company — how comfortable are you?"
This is your strongest pitch. Lean in.

"This is what I've been doing for the last three years. Every consulting engagement, I'm walking into a company that's never had a GTM Engineer before, figuring out what's broken, and shipping the first systems. I'm not coming in to define the role — I already know how to run it. The first 30 days are always the same: diagnose what's running, sit with the users, find the highest-leverage thing already half-built, ship it the rest of the way. The mistake I've learned to avoid is trying to build the long-term architecture before earning trust through quick wins. Quick wins first. Architecture second."

That answer does the work of disarming the "first-of-role risk" concern by reframing it as your specialty. Most candidates respond to that question defensively ("I'd be comfortable"). You should respond from a position of "this is literally my track record."
"Why are you interested in Tapcheck?"
This is where most candidates flunk. They reach for company-mission language ("I'm passionate about financial wellness for hourly workers") which sounds rehearsed and isn't differentiating.
The better answer is specific and operational:

"Three reasons. First, the QSR motion — multi-location franchise structures, parent-child account hierarchies, geographic TAM expansion — that's the exact pattern I've already built systems for. The work I'd do here maps directly to work I've shipped. Second, the stage — you have product-market fit, you're scaling, and the GTM engine isn't yet over-built with legacy tooling. That's the window where this work actually compounds rather than getting replatformed. Third, the team — Connor and Ron Pershad both told me what's actually broken, not just what looks good in a pitch deck. That kind of honesty is the environment where this work gets adopted. The mission matters too, but those three are what made me say yes to the process."

Ending on "the mission matters too, but..." is important. You're acknowledging the obvious thing (most candidates will lead with mission) and then telling him your real reasons. That signals senior thinking — you're not pretending to be motivated primarily by mission when what you're really evaluating is the work.
Phase 3: The Durability Test (last 5-7 minutes)
The CEO will close with questions designed to test whether you'll stay and whether you understand what you're signing up for. Likely candidates:

"Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
"What questions do you have for me?"
"Anything else I should know about you?"

The 5-year question: Have the answer ready. The version from your positioning doc works:

"Running the function I'd be joining at scale. I don't want to be the GTM Engineer who scopes the role — I want to be the one who builds it into something that compounds. Five years from now, the version I'd want to be in is one where I've built a team around me, the systems I built are still running, and the company's GTM motion is measurably more efficient because of the work. Not interested in jumping every 2 years to chase titles."

That directly addresses the durability question without being defensive about it.
The "what questions do you have for me" close: This matters. Ask 2-3, not more. Each question is signaling something about you.
The questions to ask Ron Gaver specifically:

"What would make you, 12 months from now, say this was the best hire you made all year? What would have happened?" — Forces him to articulate success criteria, gives you the answer key for the rest of the process if you advance, signals you're already thinking about impact.
"You've built something that's working — David shared that you're at a stage where warm pipeline exceeds AE capacity. What's the failure mode you're most worried about as you scale?" — Senior question. Shows you understand that growth has different problems than starting. Signals you think about second-order risks.
"What's something about the company you think you're currently wrong about and are trying to figure out?" — This is the differentiator question. Most candidates ask role questions. This is a company question. CEOs love this question because it lets them think out loud about something they're actually wrestling with. It signals you'd be a thought partner, not just a hire.

If you only ask one, ask question 1. If you have time for two, add question 3. Question 2 is for if the energy is good and you have runway.
The "anything else I should know" close: Don't fill space. If you've covered what matters, say:

"I think we covered the important things. The only thing I'd add is that I'm taking this process seriously specifically because it's the role I want — not just any role. So whatever the next step is, I'd want to be doing the work to earn it."

That's a closing line that signals conviction without desperation. Don't extend it. Stop.
